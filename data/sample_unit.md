# Chapter 6: Learning (sample text)

## 6.1 What Is Learning?

Learning is a relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge that comes from experience. It is different from reflexes and instincts, which are unlearned. A reflex is an automatic response to a specific stimulus, such as pulling a hand away from a hot stove. An instinct is a more complex inborn pattern of behavior, such as a bird building a nest without being taught.

Psychologists describe several kinds of associative learning, in which an organism links two events that happen together. In classical conditioning, the organism learns to connect two stimuli. In operant conditioning, the organism learns to connect a behavior with its consequence. A third kind, observational learning, happens by watching others rather than through direct experience.

## 6.2 Classical Conditioning

Ivan Pavlov studied digestion in dogs and noticed that the dogs began to salivate before food arrived. Food is an unconditioned stimulus because it naturally produces salivation, and salivation to food is an unconditioned response. When a neutral stimulus, such as the sound of a tone, is repeatedly paired with food, the tone becomes a conditioned stimulus. Salivation to the tone alone is then a conditioned response.

The first stage, when the pairing is formed, is called acquisition. If the conditioned stimulus is presented many times without the unconditioned stimulus, the conditioned response weakens, a process called extinction. After a rest period, a weakened response can briefly return, which is known as spontaneous recovery.

Stimulus generalization occurs when a stimulus similar to the conditioned stimulus also produces the conditioned response. Stimulus discrimination is the opposite: the organism learns to respond only to the specific conditioned stimulus and not to similar ones. Watson and Rayner's study with an infant known as Little Albert showed that fear responses can be classically conditioned in humans.

## 6.3 Operant Conditioning

B. F. Skinner studied how consequences shape voluntary behavior. Reinforcement increases the likelihood that a behavior will happen again, while punishment decreases it. The words positive and negative do not mean good and bad. Positive means something is added, and negative means something is taken away.

Positive reinforcement adds a pleasant stimulus after a behavior, such as giving praise when a child cleans their room. Negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus, such as a seatbelt alarm that stops once the belt is buckled. Positive punishment adds an unpleasant stimulus, such as a scolding. Negative punishment removes a pleasant stimulus, such as taking away a phone after a broken rule.

Shaping rewards successive approximations of a target behavior. Reinforcement can follow a continuous schedule, where every response is reinforced, or a partial schedule. Partial schedules include fixed-ratio, variable-ratio, fixed-interval, and variable-interval schedules. Behaviors learned on a variable-ratio schedule, like playing a slot machine, are especially resistant to extinction.

## 6.4 Observational Learning (Modeling)

In observational learning, people learn by watching the behavior of others, called models, and the outcomes of that behavior. Albert Bandura's Bobo doll studies showed that children who watched an adult act aggressively toward a doll were more likely to imitate that aggression.

Bandura described four steps in modeling. The observer must pay attention to the model, retain what was observed, be able to reproduce the behavior, and be motivated to perform it. Vicarious reinforcement occurs when the observer sees the model rewarded, which makes imitation more likely. Vicarious punishment occurs when the observer sees the model punished, which makes imitation less likely.
